Warning Signs of a Termite Infestation: How to Tell If You Have Termites in Your Home

The most common warning signs of termites are mud tubes on the foundation, swarmers or discarded wings near windows and doors, termite droppings near wood trim, and wood that sounds hollow. Any of these is a reason to schedule a termite inspection. This guide explains each sign of termite activity, what it does and does not tell you, and what to do next.

Termites work out of sight, so damage often goes unnoticed behind walls, under floors and inside door frames. Alabama's warm, humid weather and moisture-holding clay soil suit subterranean termites, and homes from Shelby County creek bottoms to wooded Huntsville lots and older Birmingham neighborhoods all deal with them. Knowing the early warning signs of termites can mean the difference between a routine treatment and a larger repair.

What are common warning signs of termites?

Suspected mud tubes, discarded wings, indoor swarmers and damaged wood can warrant a termite inspection. These signs are not a reliable measure of the extent of damage, and the absence of visible signs does not establish that a home is free of termites. The sections below explain how to identify termite evidence you can see yourself.

Identify termite type before you read the signs

  • Subterranean termites: the most common and most destructive in Central and North Alabama. They live in underground colonies and travel through soil or mud tubes to reach wood above ground.
  • Drywood termites: far less common inland and more often tied to the Gulf Coast or wood brought in from elsewhere. Drywood termites nest inside the wood itself and do not need soil contact.

Most termite activity in the Birmingham metro, the Huntsville area and around Lake Martin is subterranean, but a thorough inspection looks for both.

Mud tubes: the clearest early warning sign of termites

Subterranean termites build pencil-width mud tubes along foundations, piers, crawlspace walls and garage walls to travel between the soil and wood while staying out of open air. Check your foundation line, around plumbing penetrations, on brick piers and along the inside of the garage. Termites build these tubes from soil and saliva as a protected tunnel to the colony, and even an empty or broken one is worth reporting, because it shows termites were active there and may still be nearby.

Termite swarms, swarmers and discarded wings

Each spring, and sometimes after warm fall rains, mature termite colonies send out winged reproductives called swarmers to start new colonies. A termite swarm is often the first sign homeowners notice, because swarmers fly toward light and gather near windows and doors.

  • A swarm of small, winged insects coming from the soil, a wall void or wood trim.
  • Piles of termite wings on windowsills, door frames or under light fixtures; flying termites shed their wings soon after landing.
  • Swarmers do not eat wood, but termite swarms indoors suggest a mature colony is close by.

Termite swarmers are often confused with flying ants. Termites have straight antennae, two pairs of equal-length wings and a broad waist. Flying ants have bent antennae, unequal wings and a pinched waist. If you are not sure, save a few in a sealed bag and have them identified.

A pile of frass or termite droppings

Drywood termites push their waste, called frass, out of small kick-out holes. It looks like a small pile of coarse sawdust or coffee grounds near baseboards, window trim, furniture or attic joists. Subterranean termites leave behind mud and soil-packed galleries rather than loose pellets, so a pile of termite droppings points to a different problem than mud tubes do.

Hollow wood, tunnels and a sticking door frame

Termites often eat wood from the inside out and leave a thin outer shell. Tap a baseboard, door frame or floor joist; if it sounds hollow where it should not, look closer. Other signs of termite damage include:

  • Blistered or bubbling paint that looks like water damage.
  • Sagging floors or ceiling sections with no other obvious cause.
  • Doors and windows that suddenly stick.
  • Visible tunnels or galleries in exposed wood in a crawlspace or basement.

Active termite workers

Worker termites are small, pale and soft-bodied. If you break open a mud tube or a piece of damaged wood and see cream-colored insects hurrying back into the dark, you have active termites. Stop there and call a pest control professional rather than disturbing the colony further. Termites may move deeper into the wood when disturbed, which makes the colony harder to find.

What should you do if you suspect a termite problem?

Photograph the affected area if practical, avoid disturbing suspected activity and arrange an inspection. Do not rely on a household insect spray to address a suspected termite problem. EPA guidance on identifying and controlling termites gives the same advice: confirm the evidence, correct moisture problems around the home and get a professional treatment plan.

Call for an inspection if you notice any of the following:

  • Tubes anywhere on or near the foundation.
  • Discarded wings or swarmers inside the home.
  • Frass or small piles of droppings near wood.
  • Hollow-sounding wood, sticking doors or unexplained structural changes.
  • A home that has no current termite protection.

How long can a termite infestation go unnoticed?

Often months or longer. Subterranean termites can work inside walls and under flooring for a long time before damage shows. A single colony can hold many thousands of workers. That is why a home with no visible signs can still have activity, and why regular inspections and monitoring matter more than waiting for a swarm.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the first signs of termites in your home?

Mud tubes on the foundation, discarded wings near windows and doors after a swarm, and frass near wood surfaces usually show up before structural damage does.

How many termites are considered an infestation?

There is no set number. Any live termites in or on the structure, or fresh mud tubes, mean a colony is feeding nearby and should be inspected.

Can I tell if I have termites without a professional inspection?

You can spot some termite signs yourself, but much of the evidence is inside wall voids, under insulation or in crawlspaces. A trained inspector checks places most homeowners never see.

What does termite damage look like compared with water damage?

Both can cause blistered paint, soft wood and sagging floors. Termite damage usually comes with mud tubes, frass or hollow wood with internal galleries. Water damage tends to show staining, mold and a musty smell. Sometimes it is both.

Can you ever fully get rid of termites?

You can treat the colonies attacking a home and keep monitoring for new ones. Because termites live in the soil around most Alabama homes, ongoing protection matters more than a one-time fix.

Do new homes need termite control?

Yes. A pre-construction soil treatment does not last forever, and slab homes have plumbing penetrations and expansion joints that subterranean termites can use.
