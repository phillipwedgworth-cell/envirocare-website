Why do rodents enter homes in fall?

Rodents do not hibernate. When the temperature falls, a house mouse outdoors burns more energy staying warm and has less to eat, so it goes looking for warmth, steady food, and a dry nest site. Your home has all three, and this time of year it is the best warm shelter on the block. Once a mouse finds one of the wall voids near the water heater or a pile of attic insulation over a warm ceiling, there is no reason for it to leave in December or February. Food is in the kitchen, water drips under the sink, and the heat runs all night.

That is also why a winter mouse problem grows instead of fading. Inside, a heated wall is a breeding ground, mice keep breeding through the winter, and a female can produce a new litter every few weeks. If you are hearing it in the wall at night, that is not a wait-and-see situation: mice breed year-round indoors, and a female can have a new litter every few weeks.

Signs of a rodent infestation in your home in winter

You will almost never see the animal. Mice are cautious, hide by day, and travel along walls after dark, so by the time a homeowner starts seeing signs, rodent activity has usually been going on for weeks. Watch for these:

  • Sounds at night. Scratching or scurrying in the ceiling or a wall after the house goes quiet.
  • Droppings. Small dark pellets along baseboards, in drawers, behind the stove, or on pantry shelves.
  • Gnaw marks. Chewed food packaging, cardboard boxes in storage, wood trim, or the insulation on wiring.
  • Nest material. Shredded paper, fabric, or insulation in the attic, basement, or a cabinet.
  • Rub marks and odor. Dark smudges along a regular route, and a stale, musky smell in closed spaces.

Mouse or rat? The evidence tells you

What the animal leaves behind tells you which species you have. Of the species of mice in Alabama, the one in your walls is usually the house mouse. House mouse droppings are the size of a grain of rice with pointed ends, scattered wherever the mouse traveled. Norway rat droppings are three times that size, blunt, and concentrated near a runway or a food source. Roof rats, common in Alabama, especially near trees and in attics, leave droppings in between, with pointed ends, usually in the attic or on top of the wall plates. Gnaw marks tell you too: a mouse leaves tiny paired grooves; a rat leaves rough, larger tooth marks and can go through plastic, wood, and even soft metal. Grease marks along a baseboard or a rafter are a rat highway. A musky smell in an enclosed space is rats. For a side-by-side breakdown, see mice vs. rats in Alabama: which do I have?

Where they get in

A mouse fits through a gap the width of a pencil. A rat needs about a quarter, the coin. Every house in Hoover or Madison has a dozen openings that size, and they are the same on every house: the bottom corners of the garage door where the seal has lifted, the gap around the AC line set and the hose bib, the dryer vent with a broken flap, a crawlspace vent screen rusted through, weep holes in the brick, the gap under an exterior door with a worn sweep, and anywhere a branch touches the roofline and gives a roof rat a ramp to the soffit. Firewood, lumber, or dock gear stacked against siding is a covered runway to whichever of those entry points is nearest. Gaps beneath a shrub planted tight to the foundation are easy to miss. Inside, they follow plumbing and wiring holes up from the crawlspace or basement.

The Alabama specifics

Three things make rodent pressure higher here than in most states. The mild winter means the outdoor population never crashes; a January warm spell sends them right back out to forage and test your house again. The housing stock is heavy on crawlspaces and pier foundations, which give rodents a protected route to the sill plate and the plumbing chases. A damp crawlspace with standing water is especially vulnerable, to rodents and to termite activity alike. And the amount of land in row crops and pasture, around Hartselle, Harvest, and Calera especially, means that when fields are cut in September, the entire field population moves toward the nearest subdivision at once.

How to get rid of mice: why traps alone rarely end it

Traps catch the animals that are already inside. They do nothing about the opening they came through, so the population outside keeps sending replacements. A house that catches a mouse every week in November does not have a mouse problem; it has a hole. The order that works to get rid of mice is: find and seal the entry points, reduce what draws them (pet food in a sealed bin, bird seed off the garage floor, the grill drip tray cleaned), and then trap and remove what is left inside. Set snap traps along walls, where mice actually run.

Bait outside the structure, in tamper-resistant stations, handles the population before it reaches the wall. Loose bait inside a house is how you end up with a dead rat in a wall void in January, and the odor that comes with it lasts for weeks. We will not do it.

What does not work to get rid of rodents

Here is the honest answer on home remedies that promise to keep pests from entering:

  • Ultrasonic plug-in repellers. Mice get used to the sound, and it does not pass through walls. We have never seen one clear a house.
  • Peppermint oil, dryer sheets, and mothballs. A strong smell may move a mouse a few feet. It will not move a colony out of a warm wall.
  • Expanding foam by itself. Rats chew through foam. Pack gaps with copper mesh first, then seal over it with caulk or sealant.
  • Poison baits inside the walls or attic. The rodent dies where you cannot reach it. You get the smell, the flies, and still have the hole.

Cleaning up droppings

Mice and rats pose a real cleanup problem. Their droppings and urine can carry germs, including hantavirus, and rodents can bring fleas and ticks indoors with them. Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings, because that puts dust in the air. Ventilate the space first, wear gloves, wet the droppings and nest material with a disinfectant, wipe them up with paper towels, bag the waste, and wash your hands.

Prevention: what to do this week

Rodent prevention means removing the reasons and the ways to get in. These steps help prevent future unwanted visitors:

  • Walk the exterior at dusk with a flashlight. Look at every corner of the garage door, every pipe through the wall, every vent, and every crack in the foundation.
  • Seal gaps with copper mesh and sealant, not foam alone.
  • Get pet food, bird seed, and grass seed into sealed containers, and move pantry staples out of cardboard and paper.
  • Move firewood and anything stacked against the house at least a few feet off the siding.
  • Trim any branch within six feet of the roof.
  • Fix leaky pipes and drips under sinks; water keeps a winter population going.

Common questions about mice in the house in winter

Will the mice leave on their own when it warms up?

Usually not. A heated wall with food nearby beats anything outside in March. Rats and mice that have found a way in do not give it up.

Should I call an exterminator or handle it myself?

A homeowner can seal gaps and trap one or two mice. If you keep catching them or find rat droppings, it is time for professional pest control. The goal is to eliminate the way in, not to lure and catch mice forever.
